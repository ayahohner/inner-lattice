# Editorial slop check

Apply this check before showing article copy. Keep the check out of the article and out of the preview.

- Give the reader enough context to understand the first example. Do not open with an unexplained product, person, event, or hypothetical.
- Give each paragraph direction. Establish the situation, identify the error, explain the mechanism, and state the consequence in the order required by the argument.
- Do not replace exposition with definitions, examples, or conclusions. Connect each claim to the next claim.
- Do not turn feedback into an arbitrary taxonomy. Use categories only when the categories change an editorial decision.
- Do not replace a direct editing rule with an abstract label such as "referential integrity," "semantic-level collapse," "causal compression," "informational value," or "mechanical rhetoric."
- Do not show drafts, audits, self-prompts, ratings, or revision notes when the user asks for publication-ready copy.
- Do not use the checklist as an outline for the prose. A sentence-by-sentence implementation of the checklist produces mechanical writing.
- Do not patch only the latest criticized phrase. Recheck the opening, argument, terms, examples, transitions, and conclusion after every revision.
- Use a user-provided example to infer the intended abstraction. Generate examples from the abstraction instead of treating the provided example as canonical.
- Introduce an example only when the example improves understanding. State why the example matters before details that require context.
- Do not use a staged hypothetical or a matched series of examples as a substitute for explanation.
- Use established terminology when an established term is shorter and more precise. Use "implementation detail" instead of "current implementation name." Use "design constraint" when a requirement intentionally limits the implementation.
- Define a distinction by naming both sides. Do not write "the distinction," "the relationship," or "the difference" before both sides are clear.
- Repeat a technical noun when repetition prevents ambiguity. Do not use "it," "this," "that," "these," "those," or "they" for a technical referent when more than one antecedent is available.
- Assign decisions, beliefs, misunderstandings, statements, and intentions to people. Do not use a document, specification, policy, organization, discipline, or software system as a rhetorical human actor.
- Use technical verbs literally. A form records an approval; a form does not produce an approval. A specification contains a requirement; a specification does not speak.
- Keep names, labels, implementations, requirements, capabilities, outcomes, roles, and constraints separate. Do not switch levels without stating the relation.
- State the causal step between repeated terminology and replacement cost. Do not use "becomes," "spreads," "enters," or "turns into" to conceal the mechanism.
- Replace generic nouns such as "thing" and "way" with the exact noun. Remove qualifiers such as "in the practical sense" when the sentence remains true without the qualifier.
- Delete sentences that only claim importance, announce the article, praise existing fields, or validate the argument. Reject constructions such as "The remedy is not new" and "This article brings those methods together."
- Do not summarize several disciplines as unnamed authorities. Name the specific technique when the technique becomes relevant.
- Avoid survey-paper prose such as "Requirements engineering, software architecture, data modeling, and technical standards have developed ways to..."
- Avoid generic normative openings such as "A requirement should state..." Explain the observed failure before prescribing a rule.
- Avoid unsupported reader claims such as "Everyone in the conversation knows the difference."
- Avoid slogans, tidy reversals, fake punchiness, and short parallel sentences that carry less information than their cadence suggests.
- Avoid long lists unless the items form a minimum sufficient taxonomy with comparable abstraction and different consequences.
- Read the finished passage as an opening, not as an isolated paragraph. Confirm that a new reader knows the subject, the direction of the argument, and the reason to continue.
